Mediterranean, Egypt & Greece
North African and Mediterranean deployments were defined by aggressive naval patrols, intense desert warfare, and perilous sea lanes.
Deployments across North Africa and the Mediterranean basin were central to Allied strategy, securing vital oil corridors, protecting maritime trade through the Suez Canal, and denying Axis forces control of Southern Europe.[1] From complex naval patrols in heavily contested waters to widespread land campaigns across Egypt, Libya, and Greece, Allied personnel faced extreme environmental hazards and relentless combat operations.[2]

In this fourth part, we outline the complex logistics, intense desert warfare, and perilous sea lanes braved by our local servicemen, standing as a lasting tribute to the men lost across these sprawling Mediterranean battlefields. (You may select below to go directly to that section.)

1940 – 1944 Naval Patrols & Maritime Sea Lanes
Control of the Mediterranean Sea was a constant, high-stakes battle between the Royal Navy and Axis naval and air forces.[3] Escorting essential supply convoys to besieged outposts like Malta and guarding troop transports required warship crews to run harrowing gauntlets of submarine wolfpacks, magnetic minefields, and concentrated dive-bomber attacks.[3]
The ‘mid-Atlantic Gap’, where air cover was not possible.
Photo credit: Commonwealth War Graves
Cruisers, destroyers, and merchant vessels maintained aggressive escort sweeps through bomb-alley channels, enduring multi-day air raids under scorching sea conditions.[3] Operations were further complicated by sudden Mediterranean gales and stealth attacks from Italian human torpedoes and fast motor torpedo boats in narrow coastal passages.[1]
The relentless operational tempo demanded unwavering fortitude from engine room stokers, anti-aircraft gun crews, and deck hands working in enclosed spaces under extreme temperatures.[3] When ships were struck, crews faced catastrophic fires, structural collapse, and rapid sinking, often leaving survivors battling open waters far from immediate rescue assets.[2]
The hazardous naval operations across Mediterranean sea lanes are remembered through the sacrifice of Oakdale servicemen who gave their lives at sea, including William H. B. Harris.

1940 – 1944 Desert Warfare & The Greek Campaign
On land, British and Commonwealth formations engaged Axis armies in the blistering deserts of Egypt and Libya before being deployed in early 1941 to bolster the defence of Greece against invading German and Italian forces.[4]
North African & Greek Operations
Photo credit: National Army Museum
Faced with overwhelming Axis air superiority and armoured thrusts through rugged mountain passes in Greece, Allied infantry and artillery units executed desperate rearguard actions.[5] Troops fought punishing fighting retreats down the Greek peninsula, contesting every ridge and river line to buy time for chaotic beach evacuations to Crete and Egypt.[5]
In North Africa, troops navigated thousands of miles of trackless desert terrain, enduring wild temperature swings from blistering daytime heat to sub-zero night frosts.[4] Choking sandstorms ruined engines and weapons, while severe water rationing and dysentery posed as much danger as enemy anti-tank screens and strafing Luftwaffe fighters.[2]
The immense physical demands and heavy toll of land operations in Egypt, Greece, and North Africa are honoured through the memory of Oakdale servicemen including Ithel Thomas Morgan.

1944 – 1946 Post-War Greece & The Civil War
For British servicemen stationed in the Eastern Mediterranean, the withdrawal of German forces from Greece in October 1944 did not bring peace. As the Axis occupation collapsed, bitter political divisions erupted between the returning royalist government and communist-led resistance factions. Open conflict spread across many parts of the country.[6]
British forces deployed in Greece during post-war operations.
Photo credit: Imperial War Museums
Following fierce street fighting in Athens during December 1944—known as the Dekemvriana—tensions spiralled into the early stages of the Greek Civil War. British troops remained deployed across the country well beyond VE and VJ Day to secure vital ports, guard supply routes, and maintain fragile order in a fractured nation.[6]
Operating in a volatile post-war environment carried persistent hazards. Garrison units, engineers, and logistics personnel faced sniper fire, road mines, civil unrest, and the harsh realities of extended overseas duty long after their comrades elsewhere had begun demobilisation and were heading home.[7]
The often-overlooked toll of post-war duties in Greece is remembered through the sacrifice of Oakdale serviceman Frederick H. E. Pepper, who died on active service in 1946—a poignant reminder that for many British soldiers, the dangers of the Second World War did not end in 1945.
