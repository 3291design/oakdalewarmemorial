The UK Home Front
Not confined to overseas, dangerous domestic duties, bomb disposal, air defense, and training accidents deeply impacted wartime Oakdale.
While major overseas campaigns dominated public consciousness, the United Kingdom itself became a heavily militarised fortress and an active theatre of risk. Personnel stationed domestically faced hazardous duties ranging from high-risk bomb disposal and coastal defense to intensive military training exercises under wartime blackouts.[1]

In this second part, we honor the eight servicemen from Oakdale who lost their lives on British soil in direct defense of the mainland between 1940 and 1943 before completing overseas campaign star requirements.

1939 – 1943 Defense of the Realm
The constant threat of enemy bombing raids during the Blitz, combined with the urgent necessity of neutralizing unexploded ordnances, placed enormous pressure on home-service personnel. As bombs fell on British cities, specialized bomb disposal companies of the Royal Engineers worked in subterranean craters where a single slip carried fatal consequences.[2]
ATS personnel manning anti-aircraft defences, 1942
Photo credit: National Army Museum
Simultaneously, Royal Artillery anti-aircraft batteries manned searchlights and heavy guns at night, while RAF night-fighter squadrons flew interception patrols through the night skies to defend coastal ports and industrial hubs.[4][5]
Beyond frontline air defense, mobilizing millions of troops required a massive domestic infrastructure. Servicemen stationed across naval depots (such as HMS President III), Pioneer Corps construction detachments, and RAF flight schools faced severe physical demands.[3] Flight training crashes, naval instruction hazards, heavy construction accidents, and sudden illness under strained wartime housing conditions exacted a steady toll on home soil.[3]
Clearing bomb-damaged houses, London, 1940
Photo credit: National Army Museum
Between 1940 and 1943, every one of these eight local men made the supreme sacrifice on British soil while safeguarding the home front.[1] Though their service time preceded standard thresholds for overseas campaign stars, their daily devotion ensured the domestic machinery of war endured through the nation's most vulnerable hours. Brought home to rest in local chapelyards, their memory remains permanently etched into valley history.
The vital defense of the home front is remembered through the sacrifice of Oakdale servicemen who died on active service in the United Kingdom, including Lemuel George Daniels, Gwyn Davies, John Ivor Davies, William Ward Davies, Vernon Ellway, Alfred Richard Lewis, Charles Shipman, and Iorwerth Walter Watkins.
Jan. – Feb. 1941 The Blitz Comes to South Wales
For the families of Oakdale and the Sirhowy Valley, the Blitz was never a distant London headline—it unfolded on their own doorstep. As the industrial engine of the Bristol Channel, South Wales was a prime strategic target for the Luftwaffe from the summer of 1940 onward. German reconnaissance and bomber squadrons systematically hunted the region’s coal-exporting ports, railway junctions, steelworks, and oil refineries, seeking to choke off the fuel and raw materials sustaining the British war effort.[6]
Aftermath of the ten-hour Luftwaffe raid on Cardiff.
Photo credit: Wales Online
The Cardiff Blitz (2 January 1941): Cardiff’s darkest night arrived just two days into the new year. In freezing winter temperatures, more than 100 German bombers struck the city in waves over ten consecutive hours, saturating the docks and surrounding neighbourhoods with high-explosive bombs, parachute mines, and thousands of incendiaries.[6]

The human and structural toll was staggering: 165 people were killed, more than 400 were injured, and over 3,000 homes were destroyed or damaged across Grangetown, Canton, and Riverside. A single parachute mine detonated near Llandaff Cathedral, blowing off its roof and shattering the nave. Twenty miles inland, residents standing on the ridges above Oakdale and the Sirhowy Valley could see the eerie orange glow of anti-aircraft fire and burning docklands lighting up the southern horizon.
Swansea town centre after the “Three Nights’ Blitz”.
Photo credit: The Peoples Collection, Wales
Swansea’s “Three Nights’ Blitz” (19–21 February 1941): Barely six weeks later, the Luftwaffe returned in even greater force against Swansea’s shipping docks, metallurgical works, and the nearby Llandarcy oil refinery. Across three consecutive nights of relentless bombardment, German aircraft dropped more than 800 high-explosive bombs and 30,000 incendiary devices—the most concentrated air raid endured by Wales during the war.[7]

Severed water mains left fire crews battling raging infernos with dwindling water pressure as forty-one acres of the town centre were reduced to smoking rubble. By the morning of 22 February, 230 civilians and service personnel had been killed and more than 7,000 residents were left homeless.[7]
Across the Valleys, mining communities like Oakdale opened their doors to displaced families and evacuees while ARP wardens, anti-aircraft crews, and home-service units stood watch through the blackout. Together, the raids on Cardiff and Swansea underscored the harsh reality of 1941: the front line of the war ran straight across South Wales.
