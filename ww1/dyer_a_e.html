Albert Edward Dyer was a stonecutter who lived and worked in the growing industrial community of Oakdale. A widower following the loss of his wife, Mary Jane (née Samuel), he left behind his civilian life to enlist in the military, initially joining the King's Own Yorkshire Light Infantry. Already older than most recruits, he arrived on the Western Front in May 1915 and served on the battlefields of France during a period of fierce trench warfare.

As the war progressed, the physical toll of combat and his advancing age led to him being transferred to the Labour Corps. However, far from safe; these men operated directly behind the lines, frequently under enemy shellfire, to construct vital supply lines, repair shattered roads, and maintain ammunition dumps. It was during these relentless operations on the Western Front that Private Dyer suffered the severe wounds that would ultimately end his life.
Evacuated back to Great Britain for specialized medical care, he was admitted to the Manor Hill Auxiliary Hospital in Birkenhead. Despite the efforts of the medical staff, his injuries proved too severe to overcome. On 6 April 1919, at the age of 47, Private Dyer succumbed to his wounds—passing away nearly five months after the signing of the Armistice.
Following his death, he was laid to rest in the Jerusalem Chapelyard in Woodfieldside, where a permanent Commonwealth War Graves Commission headstone marks his final resting place. Funded and chosen by his children, the stone bears the moving inscription: "In loving memory of our dear Father".
Additional information taken from the Army Registers of Soldiers' Effects, 1901-1929: Albert died of wounds in Manor Hill Auxiliary Hospital, Birkenhead (a Red Cross hospital for those less seriously wounded or convalescing). His daughter, Mrs Annie Tedstone of Blackwood received pay of £35 3s and 10d and a gratuity of £24 and 10s.
