Thomas Davies
Died: 5 October 1918
Age: 23 years old
Rank: Private
Regtl. No.: 93340
Regiment: Welsh Regiment and 1st/7th Battalion Durham Light Infantry
Medals: 1914-1915 Star, Victory, British War

Thomas Davies was a Blackwood-born coal miner who left his family home at 3 Central Avenue, Oakdale, to enlist in Welsh Regiment. He was later transferred to the 1st/7th Battalion, Durham Light Infantry as Private 93340 which acted as the vital pioneer battalion for the 50th (Northumbrian) Division. Davies and his comrades were tasked with the dangerous dual role of building front-line fortifications under fire and fighting as infantrymen when the trenches were breached.

Caught directly in the path of the overwhelming German Spring Offensive, the division suffered catastrophic losses during desperate rearguard actions in France and Flanders. It was during this chaotic, fast-moving warfare that Private Davies was captured by enemy forces. He was then loaded into cattle cars and transported deep into the German Empire to the sprawling Quedlinburg Prisoner of War Camp in Saxony.
Due to the success of the Allied naval blockade, food, medicine, and basic necessities inside Germany were virtually non-existent. Prisoners were subjected to intense physical neglect and harsh labor. Weakened by months of captivity, Davies’ health collapsed. On 5 October 1918, at just 23 years of age, he died inside the camp—doomed to pass away just thirty-seven days before the signing of the Armistice would have sent him home to Oakdale.
Initially laid to rest by his fellow captives in the Quedlinburg prisoner cemetery, his final journey took place after the war. In the early 1920s, the Commonwealth War Graves Commission consolidated the graves of British soldiers who died in captivity across Germany, relocating Private Davies to his permanent resting place in Niederzwehren Cemetery in Kassel. Today, his sacrifice is permanently marked by a headstone in Germany and inscribed upon the Oakdale War Memorial back home in the valleys.
